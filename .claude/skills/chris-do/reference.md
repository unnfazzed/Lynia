# Chris Do frameworks — reference

Summarised in our own words from Chris Do's public material: The Futur podcast, YouTube,
social posts, and his *Unbland Yourself* personal-branding workbook. Several points come
from second-hand recaps of his talks. Where the wording matters, check the original at
thefutur.com. Don't quote him verbatim, and don't attribute to him words that aren't sourced.
Points marked *(general practice)* are standard brand-strategy method that The Futur
teaches alongside his own ideas. Don't present those as his inventions.

---

## POSITIONING

### 1. Who: pick your client

- **Specific beats broad.** A narrow, named audience earns more, and is easier to market
  to, than trying to serve everyone. Underpricing and weak marketing are usually a
  positioning problem, not a pricing or tactics problem.
- **Psychographics over demographics.** Describe buyers by what they believe, value,
  fear and aspire to. That gives sharper offers, clearer messages and higher prices.
- **Good clients have skin in the game.** Prefer decision-makers invested in the outcome.
  Walk away from the overly risk-averse and from buyers who only want the cheapest.
- Write an ideal-client profile: name, situation, the problem keeping them up at night,
  what they've tried, what success looks like to them, where they spend attention.

### 2. Positioning

- **Different, not better.** "Better" invites a comparison you'll lose on price.
  "Different" creates value only you can offer.
- **Own one word, one space.** A brand anchors on a single idea people associate with it.
  Owning a specific space matters more than being broadly good at many things.
- **Positioning line:** *We help [who] [achieve what] so that [why it matters].*
- **Test it.** Could a competitor say the same sentence? If yes, it isn't positioning yet.

## BRANDING

### 3. Branding

- **A brand is not a logo.** It is the gut feeling and reputation people hold about you.
  The logo and visuals are how that feeling gets recognised, not the feeling itself.
- **Unbland yourself.** A clear, consistent, fully aligned brand is the defence against
  commoditisation, AI included. Aligned means what you say, how you look and what you do
  all tell the same story. Do frames personal branding around a one-word anchor that
  everything else hangs from.
- **Story and emotion.** People remember stories and feelings, not feature lists. Make
  the customer the hero and the brand the guide.
- **Brand strategy steps** *(general practice, as taught at The Futur)*:
  1. Business goals: what the brand must achieve in 1–3 years.
  2. Audience: the ideal client from § 1.
  3. Competition: who else they could choose, and how each is positioned.
  4. Difference: the one word / space from § 2.
  5. Personality: 3–5 attributes, each with an "is / is not" pair (e.g. *warm, not cute*).
  6. Voice: how those attributes sound in a headline, an error message, a support reply.
  7. Expression: visuals, tone and behaviour that prove the attributes, used consistently.
- **Brand audit:** score every touchpoint (listing, website, app, social, support,
  packaging, the biker at the door) against the attributes. Fix the inconsistent ones.

## MARKETING

### 4. Generosity as strategy

- **Give value away first.** The Futur grew by giving its knowledge away free and selling
  depth, implementation and community later. Trust compounds.
- Teach what you know. The fear that teaching gives away the secret is mostly unfounded:
  the people who can do it themselves were never your clients.

### 5. Content

- **Value first, pitch rarely.** Most posts should teach, reframe or inspire. A minority ask.
- **Pillars.** Pick 3–5 topics the brand owns, drawn from § 2. Every post maps to one.
- **Repurpose.** One deep piece (talk, long video, article) becomes many short ones:
  clips, carousels, quotes, threads.
- **Consistency over intensity.** A steady cadence the team can keep beats bursts.
- **Measure what matters.** Saves and shares signal real usefulness. Likes are noise.

### 6. Carousels (Instagram / LinkedIn)

Do's carousels are a well-known format: a big, bold hook on slide 1, one idea per slide
in clean, highly readable type, a payoff, and one CTA.

Template (7–10 slides):
1. **Hook:** a tension, a contrarian claim or a number ("Most X fail at Y. Here's why.").
2. **Stakes:** why the reader should care right now.
3–8. **Value:** one point per slide, a short sentence plus one supporting line. Each slide
   makes the reader want the next.
9. **Payoff:** the takeaway in one line, worth saving.
10. **CTA:** one action (save, share, follow, comment a word, visit the link).

Rules: no clickbait the content can't pay off (it burns trust), lots of white space, the
same visual system on every slide.

### 7. Copy (landing pages, ads, email)

- Headline: the outcome for the specific reader. Sub-head: how, and for whom.
- Problem → why it hurts → the different way → proof → one CTA.
- Sell the hole, not the drill: outcomes and transformation, not features or hours.
- Write like you're talking to one person across a table.

### 8. Pricing the offer

- **Value-based, not hourly.** Clients pay for outcomes, not time. Price against what the
  result is worth to the client.
- **Price bracketing and anchoring.** Give a range or options, higher first, so the talk
  is about value, not a haggle over one number. Three options (good / better / best) let
  the client choose how much value they want.
- **No neediness.** Work from a "with or without you" confidence built on real skill.
  Needy sellers discount and attract bad clients.

## COMMUNICATION

### 9. Communication

- **Ask, don't pitch.** Whoever asks the questions steers the conversation. Ask until you
  understand the real problem, why it matters now, and what solving it is worth. Then
  propose. Diagnose before you prescribe.
- **Listen more than you talk.** Aim for the other side doing most of the talking. Play
  back what you heard in their own words before you answer.
- **Prepare the conversation.** Know the goal, the 3 questions you must get answered,
  and the one thing you want them to remember. Plan how to steer back if it drifts.
- **Ask why, more than once.** The first answer is the surface request. Keep asking
  until you reach the business or emotional reason underneath.
- **Present work against goals, not taste.** Restate the agreed goal and audience, then
  show how each choice serves it. Never open with "I like…".
- **Keep people informed.** Regular, proactive updates and open dialogue build alignment
  and trust. Silence is where clients start to worry. Bad news early, with a plan.
- **Say it simply.** One idea per sentence, concrete examples, no jargon. If it can't be
  said in one sentence, it isn't clear yet.
- **Conversation prep template:** goal · what they care about · 3 must-ask questions ·
  likely objections, each with a question back rather than a rebuttal · the one-line
  takeaway · the next step to agree on.

## SALES & NEGOTIATION

### 10. Sales & negotiation

- **Selling is helping someone decide.** Don't push. Find out whether there's a real
  fit, and say no when there isn't.
- **Qualify early.** Find out who decides, the budget range, the timeline and what
  happens if they do nothing. No decision-maker and no budget means no proposal yet.
- **Talk value before price.** Never name a number before you know what the outcome is
  worth to them. Then bracket (§ 8).
- **Objections are questions in disguise.** Answer "it's too expensive" with a
  question: "Compared with what?" or "What were you expecting to invest?"
- **Don't discount, trade.** If price must come down, take scope out to match. A cut
  with nothing traded teaches the client your price was padded.
- **Be willing to walk away.** That stance (§ 8, no neediness) is your leverage.
- *(General practice, often discussed on The Futur with negotiation guests:)* label
  emotions ("It sounds like timing is the worry"), mirror their last words to get them
  talking, and let silence work after you state a price.

## CLIENT RELATIONS

### 11. Client relations

- **Set expectations up front.** Scope, rounds of revisions, timeline, who gives feedback
  and how. Most client fights are expectation gaps nobody wrote down.
- **Lead, don't take orders.** Clients hire an expert to be guided. Recommend clearly,
  explain why, and let them decide.
- **Scope creep:** answer every new ask with "Happy to. Here's what it adds in time and
  cost," never a silent yes.
- **Feedback sessions:** steer comments back to the goals ("Does this serve [goal] for
  [audience]?"). Ask what problem a change is meant to fix, rather than just doing it.
- **Fire the wrong clients.** A client who drains the team, disrespects the process or
  won't pay costs more than they bring. Part ways professionally.
- Keep people informed (§ 9). Proactive updates prevent most escalations.

## BUSINESS & ENTREPRENEURSHIP

### 12. Business

- **Focus is a growth strategy.** One clear offer for one clear audience (§ 1–2) beats
  a menu of services for anyone.
- **Productise and package.** Turn repeatable work into named packages with fixed scope
  and price. They are easier to sell, deliver and price on value.
- **Build an audience, not just a client list.** Generous content (§ 4–5) brings in
  inbound demand, which beats chasing work outbound.
- **Diversify revenue only after the core works.** Services → products → education →
  community, each built on the trust the one before it earned.
- **Know your numbers** *(general practice)*: revenue, margin, cost to win a customer,
  repeat rate. Decide with them, not with vibes.
- **Hire for gaps you can't or shouldn't fill.** Write down how the work is done first,
  so the hire doesn't depend on your memory.

## LEADERSHIP & MINDSET

### 13. Leadership & mindset

- **Mindset is the first lever.** Fear of rejection, impostor feelings and a scarcity
  mindset show up as underpricing, overdelivering and saying yes to bad work. Name the
  fear before you fix the tactic.
- **Abundance over scarcity.** Generosity (§ 4), referring work out, and teaching all
  come from believing there is enough to go around.
- **Mastery takes deliberate practice.** Confidence comes from reps, not affirmations.
  Pick the skill, practise it on purpose, review the result.
- **Lead by asking.** With a team, ask questions that help people find the answer
  themselves (§ 9) rather than handing down solutions.
- **Give direct, kind feedback.** Say what worked, what didn't and why, tied to the goal.
  Say it privately and early.
- **Protect energy.** Burnout is usually a positioning, pricing or boundaries problem
  (§ 2, § 8, § 11), not a time-management one.

## DESIGN & CREATIVE DIRECTION

### 14. Design & creative direction

- **Design solves a business problem.** Judge work against the goal and the audience,
  not personal taste.
- **Critique structure:** goal → does it serve the audience? → hierarchy (what is seen
  first, second, third) → type → colour → spacing and consistency → what to change and why.
- **Clarity before cleverness.** If the message isn't instantly readable, the concept
  doesn't matter yet.
- **Typography and hierarchy carry most of the work** *(general practice)*: few
  typefaces, a clear scale, generous white space, alignment that holds.
- **Present rationale, not decoration.** Every choice ties back to the brand
  attributes (§ 3). If a choice can't be explained, question it.
- **Creative direction means setting the problem**, the constraints and the bar, then
  letting the team solve it. Don't hand them the solution.
